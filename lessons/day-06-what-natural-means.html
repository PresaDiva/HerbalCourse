1. Today's idea

"Natural" describes where a molecule came from. It does not describe what the molecule does. Your body only meets the molecule: its atoms, its shape (Lesson 1), and the targets and enzymes it reaches (Lessons 3 to 5). A receptor cannot tell whether a molecule was extracted from a leaf or built in a factory. By that reasoning, the same molecule has the same effects whatever its origin (this is my inference from Lesson 1, not a sourced claim). What can differ is the mixture around it, the dose, and the purity.

In Lesson 2 you saw why plants make these chemicals: defence. A defence chemical is built to harm something. So a plant being "natural" is no reason to expect its chemicals to be gentle. Meanwhile, some very useful medicines started in plants, which is why plants are worth studying.

Three questions replace "is it natural?":

  1. Which molecules are in it, and what do they hit?
  2. How much reaches the body (dose, form, absorption)?
  3. What does the evidence show in people, for which use?
The regulator view fits this: NCCIH says safety "depends on the specific therapy, and each complementary product or practice should be considered on its own", that "there is a lot we don't know about the safety of many of these products", and that "a manufacturer does not have to prove the safety and effectiveness of a dietary supplement" (NCCIH, Safe Use of Complementary Health Products and Practices). The page does not use the slogan "natural does not mean safe"; that is my summary of its message.

2. Evidence grade

Traditional use only for comfrey taken by mouth, and for comfrey on the skin it rests on long-standing use rather than trial results. Provisional: I opened no Tier 1 or Tier 2 source for comfrey.

The EMA's herbal committee (HMPC, Tier 3) concluded that comfrey root medicines "can be used for the relief of symptoms of minor sprains and bruises", based on long-standing traditional use. That wording means the use is accepted because it has been traditional for a long time, not because trials proved it. The EMA conditions: semi-solid products (creams, ointments) applied to the skin, "should not be taken for longer than 10 days", adults only, and no more than 0.35 microgram of pyrrolizidine alkaloids per day (EMA, Symphyti radix). The public summary was last updated 7 December 2015; a monograph revision was consulted on in 2022 and an addendum was published in March 2024, which I did not read, so current wording may differ.

I could not open a systematic review of topical comfrey (the Cochrane site returned an access error), so I make no claim about how well it works.

3. How it works

3a. The molecules

Comfrey contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids (PAs), a group of alkaloids (Lesson 2's nitrogen-containing defence chemicals). A 2021 review names comfrey (Symphytum) PAs including echimidine, symphytine, lasiocarpine, intermedine and lycopsamine, and notes that PAs from plants like comfrey can end up in herbal teas (Teschke et al. 2021, Tier 4).

3b. Harmless-looking until the liver "helps"

The harmful PAs (the ones with a particular C1-C2 double bond, called 1,2-unsaturated) are not very reactive on their own. The damage comes from your own enzymes. This is Lesson 5 turned against you:

  1. PAs reach the liver and enter liver cells (hepatocytes).
  2. CYP enzymes, mainly CYP2A6, CYP3A4 and CYP3A5, act on the PA by removing that double bond (Teschke 2021).
  3. The product is a reactive "dehydro" PA that forms adducts, meaning it sticks covalently to proteins and DNA (Teschke 2021).
  4. The damage spreads to the neighbouring cells lining the liver's tiny blood channels (sinusoids). These cells lack the key CYP enzymes, so they are hit by what the hepatocytes release, not by making the poison themselves (Teschke 2021).
  5. Blocked, injured sinusoids can cause sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS), a form of liver injury (Teschke 2021).

A prodrug is a molecule that only becomes active after the body changes it. This is the same idea in a harmful direction, sometimes called bioactivation or toxification.

3c. Supported, and not established

  • Supported (Tier 4 review): comfrey contains 1,2-unsaturated PAs, and this class is toxified by CYP enzymes to liver-damaging products.
  • Supported (Tier 3): EMA limits exposure to 0.35 microgram PA per day and restricts use to short-term skin application in adults. The existence of such a limit shows regulators treat the risk as real.
  • Not established in what I read: how much PA gets through skin from an ointment; how many cases of liver injury are linked to comfrey itself (the review I read covers PAs in general, and I read about a third of it); whether the PA content of a given comfrey product is low. Do not assume "root", "leaf", "cream" or "tea" tell you the PA level.

4. Not all plants are poisons: a counter-example

The opposite mistake is to think plant-derived means worthless. Artemisinin, a malaria drug from sweet wormwood (Artemisia annua), earned Tu Youyou half of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. I could only read news coverage of the Nobel press release (nobelprize.org returned an access error), so the detail is unverified here and does not support any grade. The pattern matters for this course: a drug that began in a traditional plant was only useful once its active molecule was identified and tested. The molecule did the work, not the label "natural".

Claim you may hearWhat to ask instead
"It's natural, so it's safe."Which molecules, what dose, and what do they do to the liver, heart or other medicines? (Comfrey PAs)
"Natural is better than synthetic."If the molecule is the same, the body responds the same (inference); compare purity, dose and evidence.
"It has been used for centuries."For what, how long per use, and was harm watched for? (EMA accepts comfrey on tradition but caps dose and days.)
"It comes from a plant, so it can't be a drug."Some drugs came from plants. What does the trial evidence say for this specific product?

5. Safety and interactions

  • Do not take comfrey by mouth, including as tea. This is my conservative advice based on the PA mechanism; I did not open a regulator page that states it, so treat it as unverified as a rule and ask a pharmacist or doctor.
  • If using a comfrey skin product, follow the EMA conditions above: adults, short-term, label-stated product. Do not apply to broken skin (my caution, unverified here).
  • People with liver disease, and anyone taking medicines processed by CYP3A4 (Lesson 5), should ask first. Interaction between comfrey PAs and other drugs was not studied in what I read.
  • Pregnancy, breastfeeding and children: EMA says adults only. I found no further data.

7. Takeaway

"Natural" tells you the source, not the safety or the benefit. Comfrey's defence molecules, the pyrrolizidine alkaloids, are turned into liver-damaging forms by the liver's own CYP enzymes, so regulators allow it only as a short-term skin product with a tiny alkaloid limit. Judge any herb by its molecules, dose and evidence.

8. Terms from today

Natural
Describes the source of a molecule or product (a plant, mineral or animal), not its safety or strength.
Synthetic
Made by chemical reactions in a lab or factory. A synthetic molecule can be identical to a natural one.
Pyrrolizidine alkaloid (PA)
A group of plant defence alkaloids; the 1,2-unsaturated ones can injure the liver.
Bioactivation (toxification)
The body converting a molecule into a more reactive, harmful form.
Prodrug
A molecule that becomes active only after the body changes it.
Adduct
A molecule stuck (covalently bound) onto a protein or DNA.
Hepatocyte
The main liver cell, where drug metabolism happens.
Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS)
Liver injury where the tiny blood channels (sinusoids) are damaged and blocked.
HMPC / EU herbal monograph
The EMA committee on herbal medicines and its written assessment of a herb's accepted uses and conditions.
Traditional use (regulatory)
Accepted for minor conditions because of long-standing use, without needing proof from trials.
