1. Today's idea

A receptor is a lock that starts a signal. An enzyme is different: it is a protein that makes a chemical reaction happen much faster, and in doing so it changes a molecule into another molecule. The molecule an enzyme works on is its substrate; the molecule that comes out is the product. The pocket where the substrate sits is the active site.

Think of a worker on an assembly line. The worker has a station shaped to hold one kind of part (the active site). A part arrives (substrate), the worker snips or joins it, and a new part leaves (product). The worker is not used up and can handle the next part straight away.

The shape idea from Lesson 1 still rules: only a substrate that fits the active site gets processed. Enzymes matter to a herbalist in three ways:

  • Plants use enzymes to make and to unlock their own chemicals. Garlic is our example today.
  • Herb molecules can block human enzymes. A blocker is an inhibitor. You met one idea of this already: aspirin-type drugs block COX enzymes (Lesson 2).
  • Enzymes break down medicines and herb molecules in your body. That is Lesson 5, and it is where many herb-drug interactions come from (Week 3).

2. Evidence grade

Mixed (preview, provisional; full re-grade in Lesson 24). Garlic supplements show small benefits for blood pressure and cholesterol in some trials, but the trials are small and varied, and evidence for colds is thin.

Ried and colleagues (2008, Tier 1) combined placebo-controlled trials of garlic preparations, mostly powdered garlic at 600 to 900 mg a day for 12 to 23 weeks. Of 25 studies in the review, 11 went into the pooled analysis. Systolic blood pressure (the top number) fell about 4.6 mm Hg more than with placebo. In people who started with high blood pressure the fall was about 8.4 mm Hg systolic (4 trials) and 7.3 mm Hg diastolic (3 trials). People with normal pressure showed no significant difference. The authors flagged small subgroups, mostly 12-week follow-up, some industry funding and moderate to high variation between trials, and asked for larger long-term trials (Ried et al., 2008; full article page opened). Note this review is from 2008; newer meta-analyses exist, which I saw only in search snippets and so do not rely on.

NCCIH (Tier 3, February 2025) says garlic supplements may lower total and LDL cholesterol "to a small extent" in people with high cholesterol, that "limited evidence" suggests a small blood pressure reduction, and that it may lower blood sugar to a small extent in people with diabetes. Eating garlic does not seem to reduce stomach cancer risk, and the effect on colorectal cancer is uncertain (NCCIH, Garlic).

For colds, a Cochrane review (Tier 1) found only one eligible trial (146 people, garlic capsule or placebo daily for three months). It suggested garlic might reduce the number of colds but concluded the evidence is insufficient (Cochrane, garlic and the common cold; summary page opened).

3. How it works

3a. A bomb with two compartments

An intact garlic clove has almost no smell. The smell and much of the chemistry begin when you crush or chop it. The key molecules, per a review of allicin chemistry (Borlinghaus et al., 2014):

  • Alliin is an unusual sulfur-containing amino acid that is inactive until the tissue is damaged.
  • Alliinase is the enzyme that hydrolyses (splits using water) alliin.
  • Splitting alliin gives allyl sulfenic acid, with dehydroalanine as a co-product. Two molecules of allyl sulfenic acid then join spontaneously into one molecule of allicin.

So allicin is made at the moment of damage, not stored. This fits Lesson 2's idea of an induced defence: a chemical weapon produced when a bite or cut happens. (That alliin and alliinase are kept apart inside the cell until damage is the usual teaching explanation, but the review I opened does not describe it, so treat it as unverified.)

3b. What allicin does next

Allicin is a reactive, sulfur-rich molecule. The review treats it mainly as a defence compound with antimicrobial activity in lab tests. How, or whether, intact allicin reaches the bloodstream after you swallow garlic is not established in the sources I opened. Many commercial supplements are made to preserve other garlic compounds, so "garlic" on a label does not mean allicin.

3c. Where proven knowledge ends

  • Supported: alliinase acts on alliin; the product reacts to form allicin; this happens upon tissue damage (Borlinghaus). Trials show modest blood pressure falls in people with hypertension (Ried).
  • Not established: which garlic molecule causes the blood pressure, cholesterol or sugar effects, and by what route. I did not open a source linking allicin or any other compound to these effects in people. This does not support the grade.
  • Unverified: that cooking or stomach acid switches off alliinase and so changes how much allicin you get. I saw this only in a search summary of low-quality pages; do not rely on it.

3d. Why enzymes matter to a herbalist

Two practical lessons. First, preparation changes chemistry: raw crushed garlic, powder, oil and aged extracts are different products and may contain different molecules. Second, the same enzyme idea works in your body: your own enzymes convert herb molecules into new ones, and some herb molecules block your enzymes. Lesson 5 follows a molecule through the body.

4. Safety and interactions

  • Fresh raw garlic on the skin "can cause severe skin irritation and chemical burns" (NCCIH). Do not put raw garlic on skin as a home remedy.
  • Oral side effects: breath and body odour, abdominal pain, gas, nausea; some people have allergic reactions. Oral garlic has been used safely in studies up to 7 years (NCCIH).
  • Garlic supplements may increase bleeding risk, which matters before surgery. NCCIH names blood thinners (anticoagulants) and aspirin as medicines to discuss with a provider.
  • Amounts above those in food may not be safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding (NCCIH).
  • Do not replace blood pressure or cholesterol medicine with garlic. Tell your prescriber if you take it.

6. Takeaway

An enzyme is a protein worker that changes a molecule it fits. Garlic uses one (alliinase) to turn alliin into allicin when it is crushed, and your body uses others to change herb molecules. Garlic supplements show modest, uncertain benefits, mostly for high blood pressure.

7. Terms from today

Enzyme
A protein that speeds up a specific chemical reaction and changes the molecule it works on.
Substrate
The molecule an enzyme works on.
Product
The molecule that results from the enzyme's reaction.
Active site
The pocket of an enzyme where the substrate fits.
Inhibitor
A molecule that blocks an enzyme from working.
Alliin
A sulfur-containing amino acid in garlic that is inactive until tissue is damaged.
Alliinase
The garlic enzyme that splits alliin.
Allicin
A reactive sulfur compound formed when alliin is split and the pieces join; the "crushed garlic" molecule.
Hydrolysis
Splitting a molecule using water.
Systolic / diastolic pressure
The top and bottom blood pressure numbers, in mm Hg.
Hypertension
Persistently high blood pressure.
Meta-analysis
A statistical pooling of results from several trials (Lesson 8 covers this in depth).
