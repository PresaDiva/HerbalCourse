1. Today's idea

Last lesson you learned to ask "what does the evidence show?" instead of "is it natural?". Today: not all evidence is equal. A friend's story and a large, well-run trial are both "evidence", but they answer different questions and can mislead in different ways.

Think of evidence as a ladder. Each rung up removes one way of fooling yourself.

What each rung can and cannot tell you

TypeWhat it isMain way it can fool you
Tradition / anecdote"My grandmother used it", "it worked for me"Most colds go away by themselves, so almost anything taken during a cold seems to work (regression to the mean: people take a remedy when symptoms are worst, and symptoms then naturally ease). Also the placebo effect: feeling better because you expect to.
Lab / animalMolecules tested on cells or animalsA dish is not a body (Lesson 5: absorption, liver metabolism). Doses used are often far beyond what a person reaches.
ObservationalResearchers record what people already do and compare outcomesConfounding: people who take herbs may also exercise more, eat better or see doctors sooner, so the herb gets credit for something else.
Randomized controlled trial (RCT)People are assigned by chance to the herb or a placebo (dummy); ideally double-blind, so neither participants nor staff know who got whichToo few people, products that vary, drop-outs, or poor design (a "risk of bias").
Systematic review / meta-analysisA method that searches for all trials on a question, judges their quality, and (sometimes) pools the numbersIf the trials are poor or too different, combining them gives a tidy answer to a messy pile ("garbage in, garbage out").

These definitions are general science-literacy knowledge; I could not open a source page that lays them out (the NCCIH pages I tried returned errors), so treat them as unverified as stated. The echinacea example below does use sourced findings.

2. Evidence grade

Mixed (preview) for echinacea and the common cold. Full re-grade in Lesson 22.

This grade rests on a Tier 1 source I opened (the Cochrane summary page) and a Tier 3 source (NCCIH, last updated November 2024):

  • The Cochrane review (published 2014, evidence to July 2013) pooled 24 double-blind randomized trials with 4,631 participants comparing echinacea with placebo: 10 trials on prevention, 15 on treatment (one trial did both). Its authors say "echinacea products have not here been shown to provide benefits for treating colds", but that there might be "a weak benefit from some Echinacea products", of "questionable clinical relevance" (Cochrane).
  • NCCIH says echinacea "may slightly reduce" the chance of catching a cold, and that it is "still unclear" whether it shortens one (NCCIH).

3. How it works

3a. Reading the evidence ladder with one herb

Echinacea is a perfect teaching case because every rung says something different:

  1. Tradition: widely used for colds. Reason to look, not proof.
  2. Lab: echinacea contains alkamides (fatty-acid-like molecules). Lab work reports that they resemble anandamide, one of the body's own cannabinoid-type messengers, and bind the CB2 receptor found mainly on immune cells (Lesson 3: a receptor is a lock). The proposal is that this may influence immune signalling, for example the inflammatory messenger TNF-alpha (search-result summary of Woelkart and Bauer 2007 and cited lab papers, Tier 4; I opened none of them in full).
  3. Trials: 24 trials, but little clear benefit (Cochrane above).

3b. Why trials disagree: the product problem

Cochrane notes the trials used products that differed in species, plant part and manufacturing method (Lesson 2: a plant is a mixture; Lesson 10 will cover extracts). Because of that "strong clinical heterogeneity" (the trials were too different from each other), the authors refused to pool the numbers for the main analysis. They also rated risk of bias: 10 trials low, 6 unclear, 8 high.

"Statistically significant" means the difference is bigger than chance alone would easily produce (details in Lesson 8). "Post hoc" means calculated after the fact, which is weaker than a planned test.

3c. Established vs not established

  • Established (Tier 1 summary): across 24 placebo-controlled trials, echinacea has not been clearly shown to help treat colds; evidence for prevention is a weak, inconsistent trend.
  • Hypothesis (Tier 4, unverified here): CB2 binding by alkamides as an immune mechanism. I did not open the papers, and the 2007 review itself says the effect cannot be clearly credited to one component. It does not support the grade.
  • Not established: which species, plant part or preparation, if any, works.

4. Safety and interactions

  • Common side effects are digestive (stomach pain, nausea). Some people have allergic reactions that "may be severe" (NCCIH, Nov 2024). Echinacea is a daisy-family plant; I did not verify whether people with daisy-family allergy are at higher risk.
  • Interaction evidence with liver-processed medicines is "conflicting" (NCCIH). Revisit with Lesson 5 (CYP) and Lesson 11. NCCIH also mentions theoretical concerns with immunosuppressants and caffeine.
  • Children: "possibly safe" short-term, but some children in a trial developed rashes. Pregnancy: limited data suggest possible safety up to 7 days in the first trimester. Breastfeeding: little known. Ask a clinician first (NCCIH).

5. Hands-on thinking exercise (not a Friday task, but try it)

A label says "clinically proven to boost immunity". Write down: (1) What type of study? (2) In people or cells? (3) Compared with placebo? (4) Which exact product? Most labels fail at least two. We practise on real papers in Lesson 8.

7. Takeaway

Stories and lab results tell you where to look; randomized, placebo-controlled trials, gathered fairly in a systematic review, tell you whether it works. Echinacea has a plausible lab mechanism but 24 trials that mostly fail to show clear benefit for colds, which is exactly why you read the highest rung you can find.

8. Terms from today

Anecdote
A personal story used as evidence.
Regression to the mean
Symptoms tend to drift back toward normal after a peak, so a remedy taken at the worst moment gets credit.
Observational study
Researchers watch what people already do, without assigning treatments.
Confounding
A hidden difference between groups (such as diet) that distorts a comparison.
Randomized controlled trial (RCT)
A study where chance decides who gets the treatment or a comparison (control).
Double-blind
Neither participants nor researchers know who received which treatment.
Systematic review
A structured search and quality check of all trials on a question. Meta-analysis is the pooling of their numbers.
Heterogeneity
Differences between studies (products, people, methods) that make pooling hard.
Risk of bias
How likely a study's design flaws are to skew its result.
Statistically significant
A difference larger than chance alone would readily produce (previewed; Lesson 8).
Post hoc
Analysis decided after seeing the data; weaker evidence.
Alkamides
Fatty-acid-like molecules in echinacea.
CB2 receptor
A cannabinoid-type receptor found mainly on immune cells.
Placebo effect
Feeling better because of expectation, not the ingredient.
