Why Plants Make Medicinal Chemicals

Example herb: white willow bark (Salix alba) and its molecule salicin. Education only, not medical advice.

1. Today's idea

Plants did not make medicine for us. A plant cannot run away from a hungry insect, a fungus or a browsing animal, so it fights with chemistry. It builds molecules that taste bad, poison, slow down or confuse whatever is eating it, or that call in the attackers' enemies. Many of the molecules in an herb that act on your body are leftovers of this arms race.

Chemists split a plant's chemistry into two groups. Primary metabolites are the molecules every plant needs to live and grow, such as sugars, amino acids and fats. Secondary metabolites are not needed for basic life. They help the plant adapt and defend itself, and primary metabolites are the raw material used to build them (Divekar et al., 2022). (A metabolite is simply a molecule made by the chemical reactions of a living thing.) Almost every herb you will study in this course is valued for its secondary metabolites.

Why this matters to you: a molecule built to act on an insect's nerves or gut can also act on a human's, because animals share many of the same proteins. That is where a plant's weapon becomes your remedy, or your poison. The dose and the person decide which.

2. Evidence grade

Promising for white willow bark extract, short-term, for low back pain in adults.

A Cochrane systematic review (Tier 1) of herbal medicine for low back pain, published in 2014, covered 14 randomized trials with 2,050 people. It concluded that willow bark extract at daily doses standardized to 120 mg or 240 mg of salicin is probably better than placebo for short-term pain and for the need for rescue medication. That finding rests on two trials with 261 people and is rated moderate certainty. A comparison with the drug rofecoxib came from one trial and is rated very low. The review found no significant adverse events in those trials but no evidence on long-term use (Oltean et al., 2014).

The European Medicines Agency's herbal committee (Tier 3) accepts dry willow bark extract for short-term lower back pain as "well-established use" and accepts minor joint pain, fever with colds and headache as "traditional use" only, because the trials were small and short (EMA, Salicis cortex). I grade it Promising rather than Strong because the best evidence is few trials, short, and the review is from 2014. Traditional claims for fever and headache are Traditional use only.

3. How it works

3a. The plant's side: a sugar-coated defence molecule

Willows and poplars make a family of defensive molecules called salicinoids, and salicin is the simplest. A 2026 review of willow chemistry (not opened in full, see unverified list) and general reviews describe salicinoids as feeding deterrents against many insects, though some specialist insects have evolved to tolerate them or even reuse them.

Salicin is a glycoside: an active part joined to a sugar (here glucose). Plants often store defence molecules this way because the sugar-bound form is less harmful to the plant's own cells and can be released when needed. (Why plants use sugar-bound storage is a general principle I did not verify against a source today; treat it as background.)

3b. The three big families

Plant defence molecules fall into three large families (Divekar et al., 2022). You will meet all three in this course.

3c. Constitutive and induced defence

Some defence molecules are always present (constitutive). Others are made only after an attack (induced). When an insect bites, the plant uses hormone signals, including jasmonic acid and salicylic acid, to switch on defence genes (Divekar et al., 2022). Two lessons for a herbalist: the amount of an active molecule in a plant varies with stress, season and growing conditions, and that is a reason standardization matters (Lesson 10).

3d. The human side: from bark to an aspirin-like molecule

EMA's summary states the chain this way: salicin is converted into salicylic acid in the body, and salicylic acid may act like aspirin by blocking cyclo-oxygenase (COX) enzymes. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction (covered fully in Lesson 4). COX enzymes build the signalling molecules that drive pain and inflammation, so blocking them lowers that signal (EMA, Salicis cortex).

  1. You take willow bark extract. Salicin arrives in the gut still attached to its sugar.
  2. Salicin is converted in the body to salicylic acid.
  3. Salicylic acid may block COX enzymes, so less pain-and-inflammation signal is made.
  4. Less signal reaches nerves, so pain may ease.

3e. Where proven knowledge ends

  • Supported: willow bark extract standardized to salicin probably reduces short-term low back pain more than placebo (Cochrane, moderate certainty).
  • Hypothesis: that salicin is the reason. EMA itself says the amount of salicylic acid produced from salicin is small and the effect may come from other components of the bark. So "willow bark is natural aspirin" is an oversimplification. Willow bark contains many molecules (including flavonoids and other phenolics), and the Cochrane trials used a whole extract, not pure salicin. Which molecules do the work is unresolved.
  • Cochrane also reported that willow bark had only a minimal effect on platelet clumping (the blood-clotting step aspirin blocks) compared with a heart-protective aspirin dose, from one trial of 51 people. Do not treat willow bark as a substitute for aspirin taken for the heart.

4. Safety and interactions

  • EMA says willow bark products must not be used by people who are allergic to salicylates (such as aspirin) or to NSAIDs; people with salicylate-sensitive asthma, active stomach ulcers, severe liver or kidney disease, clotting disorders or G6PD deficiency; women in the third trimester of pregnancy; and children and adolescents (EMA, Salicis cortex).
  • Adults only, and for back or joint pain no longer than 4 weeks without medical advice. See a doctor if fever lasts over 3 days or a headache over 1 day.
  • Long-term safety is not established (Cochrane).
  • Taking willow bark together with aspirin, other NSAIDs or blood thinners may stack similar effects. I did not open a source on specific interactions; ask a pharmacist. Interaction mechanisms are covered in Lesson 11.

6. Takeaway

Herbal molecules exist because plants use them as weapons and signals; they help or harm us when they happen to fit our own proteins, so the question is always which molecule, how much, and for whom.

7. Terms from today

Metabolite
A molecule made by the chemical reactions of a living thing.
Primary metabolite
A molecule every plant needs for basic growth and life (sugars, amino acids, fats).
Secondary metabolite
A molecule not needed for basic life but used for defence and adaptation; most herbal actives are these.
Terpenoids / Phenolics / Alkaloids
The three big families of secondary metabolites (see table).
Constitutive vs induced defence
Always present versus made only after an attack.
Glycoside
A molecule made of an active part joined to a sugar.
Salicin / Salicylic acid
Salicin is willow's sugar-bound defence molecule; salicylic acid is the aspirin-like molecule it is converted to in the body.
Enzyme
A protein that speeds up one specific chemical reaction (previewed; Lesson 4).
COX (cyclo-oxygenase)
Enzymes that make signalling molecules driving pain and inflammation; aspirin-type drugs block them.
NSAID
Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, such as ibuprofen or aspirin.
Standardized extract
An extract adjusted to a stated amount of a marker molecule (previewed; Lesson 10).
Systematic review
A study that gathers and combines all trials on a question using fixed rules.
Randomized controlled trial
A study where people are randomly assigned to treatment or comparison.
