1. Today's idea

In Lesson 1 you met the idea that shape decides what a molecule does. Today we name the "lock". A receptor is a protein, usually in the outer membrane of a cell, with a pocket that a particular molecule fits into. When the right molecule sits in the pocket, the protein changes shape and starts a signal inside the cell. The molecule that fits is called a ligand (from the Latin for "to bind").

Think of a doorbell. The button (the receptor) sits on the outside of the house. Pressing it (the ligand arriving) does nothing to the button itself, but a signal runs inside and someone answers the door. Your cells use receptors to hear hormones, nerve messengers and, by accident, plant chemicals.

Ligands come in two main kinds, and you will use these words for the rest of the course:

  • An agonist fits the pocket and switches the receptor on.
  • An antagonist fits the pocket but does not switch it on, and blocks the real ligand from getting in. (This is a general pharmacology definition; today's herb is an agonist.)

Last lesson, Day 1's menthol opened an ion channel called TRPM8. Today's example works on a close relative of it.

2. Evidence grade

Promising for the prescription 8% capsaicin patch, for nerve pain after shingles. Mixed for over-the-counter low-concentration capsaicin creams.

A Cochrane review (Tier 1) of the 8% capsaicin patch covered 8 randomized, placebo-controlled studies with 2,488 adults who had moderate or severe long-lasting nerve pain. For postherpetic neuralgia (nerve pain that lingers after shingles; 4 studies, 1,272 people) about 10% more patch users than controls reported being much or very much improved, a number needed to treat of 8.8 at 8 weeks. The reviewers rated this moderate quality and said it gives moderate pain relief to a minority of people. For HIV neuropathy and painful diabetic neuropathy the certainty was very low, and for pain after hernia surgery there was no clear benefit. Short-lived burning, redness and pain at the patch site were common; serious adverse events were 3.5% with capsaicin and 3.2% with control (Derry et al., 2017; summary page opened, full text not read).

Memorial Sloan Kettering's integrative medicine summary (Tier 3) says the patch is best used when other therapies have failed, because few patients benefit. It says low-concentration capsaicin is unlikely to be meaningfully useful for neuropathic pain, is conditionally recommended for osteoarthritis (a meta-analysis suggests it may be comparable to topical NSAIDs), and has inconclusive evidence for rheumatoid arthritis (MSK, Capsaicin, updated March 2023).

This is a preview grade. Capsaicin gets its full lesson in Lesson 20, where cream strengths and osteoarthritis trials will be examined.

3. How it works

3a. The molecule and its lock

Capsaicin is the compound that makes chili and cayenne fruit hot (MSK). It is a ligand for a receptor called TRPV1. TRPV1 is also an ion channel (Lesson 1): a protein that forms a gate through the cell membrane. Here the receptor and the gate are the same protein. When capsaicin binds, the gate opens. A 2016 review says capsaicin binds in a region spanning transmembrane domains 3 and 4 (parts of the protein that cross the membrane) (Smutzer and Devassy, 2016).

TRPV1 sits on the endings of sensory nerve fibres that sense pain and heat. It opens in response to more than capsaicin. The same review reports that it opens when temperature rises to about 43 °C and when acidity is high (pH below 6.0). That is why chili feels like burning heat: the receptor cannot tell capsaicin from real heat, so it sends the same "hot" message to your brain. Nothing is actually burned.

3b. The chain of events

  1. Capsaicin reaches the endings of pain-sensing nerve fibres (nociceptors) and binds TRPV1.
  2. The channel opens and positively charged ions flow in, with a tenfold preference for calcium (Smutzer and Devassy). The sudden charge change in the nerve ending is called depolarization.
  3. The nerve fires and releases substance P, a messenger that transmits pain signals (MSK). This is the burning you feel first.
  4. With repeated or high-dose exposure, the nerve ending stops responding. MSK describes this as reversible "defunctionalization" of sensory nerve endings, and says pain subsides as substance P is depleted. The 2016 review adds that desensitization depends on calcium and takes several minutes, so it is different from ordinary adaptation.
  5. With less signalling from those endings, pain from them may ease. This is the reason a burning compound can be used for pain relief.

3c. Where proven knowledge ends

  • Supported: capsaicin is an agonist at TRPV1; TRPV1 also responds to heat and acid; opening lets calcium in; the 8% patch gives moderate relief to a minority of people with postherpetic neuralgia (Cochrane, moderate certainty).
  • Mechanism accepted but stated at a general level in the sources I opened: that "defunctionalization" and substance P depletion explain the pain relief in people. MSK describes it, but I did not open a trial that measures it in patients.
  • Not established: cancer effects. MSK says there is continued controversy over whether capsaicin is a carcinogen, co-carcinogen or anti-carcinogen. Lab studies and mouse studies disagree. This is not a reason to avoid chili in food; it means topical long-term use is not proven safe or unsafe in this respect.

3d. Why receptors matter to a herbalist

Two practical lessons. First, a receptor does not know where a molecule came from; a plant molecule that fits acts, and the same molecule can mean different things in different tissues where the receptor is found. Second, because the effect depends on how many receptors are bound, dose and product strength matter, which is why a 0.025% cream and an 8% patch are different tools (the patch is applied in a clinic).

4. Safety and interactions

  • Capsaicin can irritate mucous membranes, the eyes and broken skin. Avoid irritated, infected or wounded skin (MSK). Wash hands after use and do not touch your eyes.
  • Common topical effects: burning, redness, swelling, dryness, soreness, itching, cough (MSK).
  • Case reports describe heart problems (coronary vasospasm, heart attack) and serious application-site burns after the patch. MSK lists unstable or poorly controlled high blood pressure and recent heart or stroke events as contraindications for the patch.
  • One reported case of cough with an ACE inhibitor (a blood-pressure drug) and topical capsaicin. Animal data suggest capsaicin may inhibit the liver enzyme CYP2C19 and induce CYP3A4, but MSK says clinical relevance is not determined. Enzymes are covered in Lesson 4.
  • I did not open a source on use in pregnancy or breastfeeding or in children. Ask a pharmacist.

6. Takeaway

A receptor is a protein lock on a cell; a herbal molecule works when it fits and either turns the lock on (agonist) or blocks it (antagonist). Capsaicin turns on TRPV1, the body's heat-and-pain gate, and with repeated exposure that nerve ending goes quiet.

7. Terms from today

Receptor
A protein, usually in a cell's outer membrane, with a pocket that a particular molecule fits; binding starts a signal.
Ligand
Any molecule that binds to a receptor.
Agonist
A ligand that switches a receptor on.
Antagonist
A ligand that fits but does not switch the receptor on, and blocks other ligands.
TRPV1
A receptor and ion channel on pain-sensing nerve endings, opened by capsaicin, heat above about 43 °C and acid.
Nociceptor
A nerve ending that senses painful stimuli.
Depolarization
The electrical change in a nerve ending when positive ions flow in, which can make it fire.
Substance P
A messenger released by sensory nerves that helps transmit pain.
Desensitization / defunctionalization
A nerve ending responding less after repeated or strong capsaicin exposure; reversible.
Postherpetic neuralgia
Nerve pain that lingers after shingles.
Number needed to treat (NNT)
How many people must be treated for one extra person to benefit; lower is better. (Previewed; Lesson 8.)
